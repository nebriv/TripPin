How to read this document

MUST and MUST NOT are hard — break one and the work comes back. SHOULD is a strong default you may argue with. MAY is yours.

01 What this is

TripPin is a daily guessing game for five specific people. Not a product. Not a startup. There is no signup, no marketing page, no growth loop. There are five friends — Ben, Benjamin, Andrew, Maddie, Anna — and a private URL behind a shared password.

They pooled their real Airbnb trip histories. Every day the game deals three of those stays to everybody, and you work out who was there and where it was.

The joke at the centre of it

It is a game about the comedy of your friends' taste.

The photos are real Airbnb listing photos, which means they are mostly mundane. A beige living room in Lima. A bunk bed in Queens with a bedspread nobody chose. A tiny home upstate at golden hour, because that host knew what they were doing. They are not beautiful travel photography and they MUST NOT be designed as if they were.

The amenities are the punchline. These are real strings from real listings:

Private hot tub — available all year, open 24 hours
29 inch TV with standard cable
Private backyard — Not fully fenced
Fast wifi — 73 Mbps
Ski-in/Ski-out
Rifle locker

"29 inch TV with standard cable" tells you something about a place and about the person who booked it. "Wifi" does not. The game already ranks amenities by how weird and specific they are and leads with the strangest. That ranked oddity is the personality of the product. If your design buries it, the design has failed.

Tone

Dry, warm, a little deadpan. The register of a group chat between people who have known each other fifteen years, not the register of a consumer app.

It should feel like evidence being laid out, not like a quiz being administered. You are looking at a photograph of a room and inferring a human being from it. Detective energy, played for laughs.

Things it is not: perky, gamified, congratulatory, "Well done!", badge-driven, streak-anxiety-inducing, corporate-travel, real-estate-listing, or wistful travel-blog. It also MUST NOT read as an Airbnb product or sub-brand.

Design for the 400th play, not the first

Everyone already knows the rules. Nobody will ever onboard again. The interface that matters is the one you see every morning for two years.

  • Density over hand-holding
  • No tutorial UI in the main flow — there is a help modal, and it is for reference
  • Nothing charming once and irritating on the fortieth repeat
  • The first frame after load is the puzzle, immediately

02 How it plays

One turn, two answers, then a reveal. Three turns a day.

┌─ THE CARD ─────────────┐ ┌─ THE MAP ───────────────────┐ │ photo (hero) │ │ │ │ photo strip (3 thumbs) │ │ world map, pan + zoom │ │ ♨ the odd amenity │ │ tap to drop a pin │ │ [chip] [chip] [chip] │ │ │ │ 2 guests · 1br · 1 bed │ └─────────────────────────────┘ │ ★ 4.96 (190) │ └────────────────────────┘ ① WHO WAS THERE? tap everyone [Ben] [Benjamin] [Andrew] [Maddie] [Anna] [ Lock it in ]
  1. Who was there? Multi-select over the five. Partial credit. Most stays are one or two people; some are four. Over-guessing is penalised.
  2. Where was it? Drop a pin on a world map. Scored on great-circle distance.
  3. Lock it in — enabled only when both halves are answered.
  4. Reveal — the map flies to frame your pin and the true location with a dashed line between them; the card is replaced by the result panel.
  5. Next stay → repeat → after the third, the day's summary.

Hard requirement

Both answers belong to one turn. They MUST feel simultaneous and equal, not sequential steps. The player's eye moves photo → people → map → button.

Sharing

A Wordle-style emoji grid, copied to the clipboard. This is the only thing that ever leaves the product, so it MUST stay parseable as plain text in a message:

TripPin #14  2,140/3,000
✅ 🟩🟩🟩🟩⬜  12 mi
🔶 🟨🟨🟨⬜⬜  240 mi
❌ 🟧🟧⬜⬜⬜  610 mi
🎯 best 12 mi · 🌍 862 mi off · 🔥6
https://…/?k=…

One line per stay. First glyph is the WHO result — exact, partial, nothing. Then a five-square proximity bar, Worldle-style: how much of the distance score you kept, one square per fifth, coloured by the band you landed in. Then the raw distance. The tail carries your best pin, your total miles off, and the streak.

Note what it withholds. Distances and bands, never a place — so you can post it in the group chat before anyone else has played.

You MAY redesign the glyph vocabulary and the tail. You MUST keep it to one line per stay, plain text, and free of anything that identifies a location.

03 Scoring

Where0–800. 800 · e^(−miles / 600). Inside 15 miles is a flat 800.
Who0–200, by set overlap (Jaccard). Exact party = 200.
Per stay1,000
Per day3,000

These four bands need a semantic colour ramp separate from your brand accent, legible in both themes. Do not reuse the accent for "good".

04 The pages

Four pages, different audiences, different rules.

All five · daily

/ — the game

The main event. Everything in §05 lives here. States: gate, playing, revealed, day-complete, and empty.

The four friends · a handful of times ever

/import — adding your trips

A five-step instructional flow that asks a non-technical person to open Chrome DevTools and paste a console snippet. Intimidating; the design's whole job is making it feel routine. Afterwards it lists only that person's own stays for fixing a wrong town or adding a story — nobody sees anyone else's data.

One person · occasionally

/admin — counts and health

Four total tiles, a per-player table, an editor for the five "tell" lines, and one quarantined destructive action that must look different from everything near it.

One laptop · never deployed

editor.html — full data editor

Lowest priority. Style it consistently and move on. Do not spend budget here.

Hard requirement · admin

The admin page MUST NOT display place names, coordinates, photos or stories. The person running the game also plays it and cannot be allowed to spoil themselves by opening the dashboard. This catches people out, because dashboards normally want to show you your data.

05 Components

This is the real work. Every one of these exists today and needs redesigning.

5.1 The stay card — the centrepiece

The single most important object in the product. Composition, top to bottom:

  1. Hero photo. Arbitrary aspect ratio, arbitrary quality, frequently unflattering. Currently a 16:7 crop. Your design MUST survive a genuinely ugly photo — assume a dim phone snap of a beige sofa, because that is a real card.
  2. Property-type badge over the photo: "Tiny home", "Entire cabin", "Entire rental unit".
  3. Photo strip — up to three more thumbnails. Clicking one swaps it into the hero. These are CDN links that can fail; a failed thumb removes itself, so the strip MUST look correct with 3, 2, 1 or 0 thumbs.
  4. The odd amenity, alone on its own line with an icon, at display size. The most characterful line on the page.
  5. Amenity chips — up to five more, ranked, less weird than the first.
  6. Facts line, demoted: 2 guests · 1 bedroom · 1 bed · 1 bath.
  7. Rating ★ 4.96 (190), and nightly price when present.

There is no title. There is no date. See §06.

Hard requirement · empty states

Empty is the norm, not the exception. 21 of the 41 current stays have no amenities at all, because enrichment could not find their listing. A card can be nothing but a photo and "4 nights". That version MUST still look deliberate — not like a broken card.

5.2 The who-picker

Five multi-select toggles. Each is an avatar — coloured circle, single initial — plus a first name. Two of them are "B", Ben and Benjamin, so the initial alone is never sufficient; the name is load-bearing.

Needs unselected, selected, hover and focus-visible states, and a clear affordance that this is multi-select rather than radio. Must fit five across on desktop without one orphan wrapping, and lay out sanely at 375px.

#3d7a8c Ben #8c4a6b Benjamin #5c7a4a Andrew #b07c2e Maddie #4a5c8c Anna

Current player colours — yours to replace, but keep five distinguishable hues that work on both grounds.

5.3 The map

Hard requirement

The map is a bespoke SVG renderer and you MUST NOT replace it. No Leaflet, no Mapbox, no tiles. It is 446 KB of baked Natural Earth geometry with zero network calls, which is a deliberate feature. You SHOULD restyle every part of it.

What it does:

  • Two projections on a toggle: Equal Earth (default — genuinely equal-area, so the world is a rounded lens, not a rectangle) and Mercator (rectangular, easier to hit the Arctic). Your styling must work for both silhouettes.
  • Pan, wheel and pinch zoom, double-click zoom, keyboard arrows, reset
  • 6,776 city labels fading in progressively with zoom, with collision avoidance
  • US states and Canadian provinces above a zoom threshold
  • Two pin types — the player's guess, and the answer, which carries a place-name label pill

Constraints: labels are HTML positioned over the SVG so they never scale; strokes are non-scaling. Controls currently sit bottom-right as a stack of four 28px buttons — zoom in, zoom out, reset, projection.

5.4 The reveal panel

Replaces the card on lock-in:

  • Distance headline — 316 mi away, or Bullseye under 15 miles
  • Points earned +472
  • A meter bar filling to the WHERE fraction, coloured by band
  • Two result rows — WHERE (true place, month, points) and WHO (a tag per person who was actually there, marked hit or miss, plus struck-through tags for anyone you named who was not)
  • "Plus 12 other people who are not playing" when the party included non-players
  • The story — a free-text anecdote the owner wrote. This is the emotional payoff of the whole turn and currently the most under-designed thing in the product. Set it at display size.
  • Next stay →

5.5 Progress pips

One per stay in the day. Upcoming, current, and done — where done is tinted by distance band and carries a secondary mark for the WHO result. Three tiny objects each carrying two dimensions of information.

5.6 Action bar

Fixed to the bottom. A hint line that changes with state ("Pick everyone who was there, then drop a pin" → "Now drop a pin on the map" → "Ready — two people") plus the primary button. Hidden during reveal and summary.

5.7 Day summary

Score against max; a one-line grade that is dry rather than congratulatory ("Roughly the right planet."); a row per stay with place, distance, who and points; share button; practice button; four stat tiles; and a live countdown to the next set.

5.8 Supporting

Help modal — rules, the score key, and the five "tells", one line per person describing what kind of place they always book. Stats modal with a sparkline of recent days. Inline messages. The gate screen.

06 Anti-spoiler rules

These are the constraints most likely to be violated by a good designer doing what normally makes a card better. Read them twice.

Must not appear on the playing card

  • The listing title. Host-written titles routinely contain the town — "Cosy Cabin in Asheville" hands over the answer. Titles are stripped entirely.
  • The date. The month is half the answer: February plus snow means the Arctic trip. Dates appear only in the reveal.
  • The place name — including inside amenity text or a review quote, which is why a redaction pass blanks any part of the answer appearing in displayed strings, rendering it as •••. If your design introduces a new text surface on the card, it must go through that redaction.

07 Technical requirements

  • No build step, no framework. Vanilla JS, plain CSS. The whole thing inlines into single HTML files via a Python script. React, Tailwind, Sass, PostCSS and npm are all out.
  • CSS custom properties for everything. There is an existing token set (--bg --panel --ink --muted --faint --line --accent --good --ok --meh --bad --sea --land --land-line). You may rename and restructure it; you MUST deliver a token system, not hard-coded values.
  • Both themes. System-following by default, with an explicit override winning in both directions. Every colour defined at :root first.
  • Mobile-first, and genuinely used on phones. The card and the map both have to be reachable in one turn. The current build makes you scroll past a 583px card to reach the map on a 375px screen — fixing that is in scope and is one of the clearest wins available to you.
  • Accessible. Visible focus states; the map is keyboard-operable (arrows pan, +/− zoom, Enter drops a pin); aria-pressed on toggles; prefers-reduced-motion honoured; real contrast in both themes.
  • Fonts from Google Fonts or system stacks only.
  • Photos are <img> from a CDN and can fail. Every image needs a defined failure appearance.

08 What's wrong with it now

The current design is a competent NYT-daily-games pastiche: warm cream paper, a serif display face, terracotta accent. It works. It is also exactly the design anyone would produce from that reference, and it carries none of the specific comedy of the actual content.

  1. The story is wasted. The best content in the product — the anecdote after the guess — is a small paragraph in a bordered box.
  2. The card doesn't know what to do with a bad photo, and most photos are bad.
  3. Mobile makes the two answers feel like two screens.
  4. The five-person picker looks like a form control, not like accusing your friends of something.
  5. No identity. No logo, no mark, no favicon beyond an emoji — nothing that makes the share grid recognisably this game when it lands in a chat.

09 Art direction

Ideas, not instructions. Yours to reject — offered so you know the register.

  • The material world of the subject: listing photos, map contours, pin drops, lockboxes, keypads, a printed itinerary, a guestbook, luggage tags, a polaroid's white border, a corkboard with red string. The corkboard in particular fits the "who was there" half.
  • Something that treats the photo as evidence — a border, a caption slug, an exhibit number, a slightly clinical frame that makes a beige sofa feel like it is being examined.
  • The map is the one place that MAY be beautiful and atmospheric. It is already the most striking thing on screen.
  • Avoid: travel-brochure gradients, passport stamps, paper-plane icons, dotted flight paths, globes with orbit rings, and the entire visual language of booking sites.
  • We already know we don't want warm cream + serif + terracotta — that is what we have — or near-black with one acid-green pop.

Take one real swing. Five people will see this every morning for two years. A safe design is worse than a strange one.

10 Deliverables

  1. Identity — wordmark, standalone mark usable at favicon size, and a favicon. The mark must still read at 16px in a browser tab.
  2. Design language — documented token system (colour in both themes, type scale, spacing scale, radii, elevation, motion), with rationale.
  3. Component designs — every item in §05, in every state listed.
  4. Page designs — the four pages in §04 at 375px and 1280px minimum, covering every state in 4.1.
  5. The share grid — glyph vocabulary, and how it reads in a message.
  6. Implementation notes — enough that a developer can map your design onto the existing class names without guessing.

11 Acceptance criteria

  • A card with only a photo and "4 nights" looks deliberate.
  • A card with an ugly photo looks deliberate.
  • On a 375px phone a player completes one turn — both answers — without feeling the two halves are separate screens.
  • Both themes pass contrast, and neither looks like an afterthought.
  • Nothing on the playing card or the admin page violates §06.
  • The share grid is recognisable as this game in a group chat.
  • Someone who has played 400 times is neither bored nor irritated by any animation in it.
